ECOLOGY / HABITAT SUPPORT

More connected habitat. More feeding opportunities.

The nine species counters show suitable habitat tiles, not animal populations. Choose Ecology, then a species, to highlight exactly the same habitat used by its counter. Teal marks suitable tiles; in food-web view, teal is shrimp nursery habitat and gold marks supported wading-bird feeding areas.

Marsh flood protection

Healthy, connected marsh reduces the strength of water moving across it in this game. Larger patches and more marsh along a flood route give more buffering. Nutria grazing and modeled vegetation stress weaken it; losing marsh removes it. The strongest remaining route wins, so water can bypass marsh through open channels. Protection is limited and can be overwhelmed. Blue P tiles are a comparison with the same event and terrain but no marsh attenuation; they are not guaranteed safe in real life.

The qualitative idea is informed by EPA on wetlands storing and slowing floodwater. All attenuation strengths and patch thresholds here are invented game settings.

The first food-web rule

A marsh tile without nutria becomes a wading-bird feeding area when at least 3 shrimp nursery water tiles are reachable within 2 tile steps through water. Levees and intervening land block the water paths. Hyacinth excludes a water tile from the shrimp nursery count. Losing qualifying nursery habitat can switch the feeding area off immediately.

The 3-tile threshold and 2-step range are invented game settings, not biological measurements. This models potential feeding habitat: it does not create three shrimp, spawn a bird, consume prey, or change the general bird habitat counter. Real predator abundance also depends on prey density, depth, access, nesting habitat, seasons and time lags.

Ecological context: NOAA on shrimp nursery habitat; National Park Service on wading birds eating shrimp and other aquatic prey. The game groups species and simplifies these relationships.

PUBLIC FUNDING / GAME POLICY

Fund a different future.

Grant terms and allocations are invented for gameplay. Only work performed after application counts. Completion is reviewed after four seasons; the advance is retained if a project falls short. Timber is fast, unrestricted income, with an immediate habitat cost. Opening this window pauses the clock.

FIELD GUIDE / FIRST PASS

Care, consequence, and another season.

Choose one role and one real-location map. The event seed does not generate or alter its terrain. Time runs continuously while Play is active: one season takes 60 seconds at 1× speed, or less at 2×/4×. Pause to plan. Effort capacity scales with map size: one point per 45 tiles, rounded up, with a minimum of 8. These 1,080-tile maps hold 24 effort. A full reserve recovers over two game minutes: +1 every 5 game seconds here, faster in wall time at 2×/4×. The visible bar and countdown show available effort and the next refill. Recovery stops while paused and never exceeds capacity. Restoration and construction use 1 effort; cutting mature timber uses only 0.5 effort and earns 3 funds, immediately reducing forest and bird habitat. Actions also need funds. Local funding adds 3 funds each season, and the state allocation adds 12 each spring. Fishing, gardens, and new undamaged homes add income. Goals are optional milestones; play continues after you meet them.

Where the terrain comes from

The three map windows are Barataria Preserve, Lake Cataouatche, and Jean Lafitte, Louisiana. NOAA’s 2021 C-CAP land-cover class is sampled at each coarse tile center; Census 2025 water polygons override mapped water cells. USACE levee/floodwall alignments are retained at tile scale. Forested wetlands use the forest symbol; emergent and scrub wetlands use marsh. Other open land is grouped under the meadow symbol. Unconsolidated shore is not necessarily sand. A map window is not an administrative boundary.

These mixed-vintage sources are not a current field survey. Coarse tiles can miss small features, and a defense within a mixed developed/water cell may be shown as context without blocking the whole cell. Original source classes and coordinates remain visible in Tile Notes. Initial tree ages and elevation are game assumptions, not remote-sensing measurements. Real salinity and flood forecasts are not provided.

Two views of the same place

Switch between Plan and Isometric without changing the game. Both views use the same reusable pixel-art set: cypress trees, reeds, cottages, earthen levees, hyacinth and wildlife. These objects represent coarse land-cover patches, not surveyed trees or buildings. The shallow cutaway is a drawing convention, not measured elevation. North points up in Plan and toward the upper right in Isometric. Use + to zoom in, then scroll to explore, or Fit map to see the whole landscape. Click a tile’s ground footprint in either view; tall artwork is decorative. The cursor, tool, forecast, salinity layer, undo and saved game are shared. Arrow keys follow the original map rows and columns in both views.

Reading the pixel atlas

Water is blue; cypress trees represent forest, tufts represent marsh, cottages represent development, and grassy embankments represent levees. Small brown dots show accumulating silt. Both views use the same object artwork. The salinity layer uses outlined, half-filled, or filled blue circles for fresh, brackish, and salty water. Landscape and habitat metrics stay visible with the map; the latest action shows changes immediately. Journal and Map key hold the longer explanations.

Marsh is protective infrastructure

Marsh provides habitat and reduces modeled overland flood exposure. Water loses strength as it crosses marsh; larger connected patches buffer more strongly. Grazing and modeled vegetation stress weaken that effect. Marsh itself can be wet while protecting land farther inland. Open-water bypasses retain their strength, and severe events can overwhelm the buffer. This game does not model storage volumes, water speed or real flood depths.

Choose Ecology → Marsh flood protection. Teal marks marsh; blue P marks tiles that would flood in the same forecast without marsh buffering but stay dry with it. The forecast reports buffered developed cells, not individual households. If the next event is mild, this count can be zero. In the protection layer, Heavy rain test and Extreme water test preview other event strengths without changing your forecast, clock or saved landscape. Restoring marsh takes time; removing a levee can increase exposure before the new marsh establishes.

Stakes you can recover from

The next event is forecast before you commit. Amber marks ground at risk of flooding—not guaranteed home damage. Levees block flood spread, but high-water events wear them down after the event. A levee that fails opens the path for following seasons. Repair a levee before it fails. Flooded homes can be damaged and lose income until repaired. A developer’s raised foundation has extra elevation. Mature forest tolerates short floods in this game; newly planted trees are more vulnerable.

Hyacinth arrives randomly in fresh river water and can spread to adjacent fresh water each season. It blocks fishing habitat until cleared. Nutria sightings can lead to marsh grazing. These simplified effects are inspired by real ecology; their probabilities and timing are invented for this game.

Selective timber and replanting

Harvesting a mature forest tile costs 0.5 effort and returns 3 funds. It leaves meadow, reducing forest and associated wildlife habitat immediately. Planted trees need three seasons to become harvestable. The Forester’s grove ability can replant up to three nearby eligible tiles efficiently, but young trees remain vulnerable to high-water events. All roles can harvest timber; the Forester’s charter still requires a net gain in forest.

Growth and water

Placed sediment becomes mud, then marsh after three non-salty seasons. Fresh modeled channel water next to marsh can accumulate six seasons of silt and turn to mud. This may narrow a channel. Four salty seasons turn marsh to mud. Fresh/coastal influences use assumed northern/southern boundary water cells; they are not measured inflows. Moisture reaches up to two tiles from water, blocked by levees. Flooding spreads from connected water onto land below the event’s water level. There is no calibrated hydrodynamic or population model.

Role abilities cost 1 effort, have a three-season cooldown, and use the displayed fund cost. Foresters, ecologists and engineers act on up to three neighboring eligible tiles. The fisher harvests up to three ready tiles. You must choose an eligible center tile.

Arrow keys move the tile cursor; Enter or Space applies the active tool. Inspection is free. Undo reverses the most recent action until the season advances, preserving elapsed time and effort recovery. Saved games reopen paused; there is no offline catch-up. Opening a reference or grant window pauses play. Grant advances give 6 funds immediately; keeping 3 qualifying project tiles through a four-season review earns 8 more. Planted trees must be at least 3 seasons old. One application per year; grant values and timing are invented game policy. Your real-place game saves in this browser automatically; fictional-map saves are retained separately and are not loaded here; a new island replaces it only after you choose to begin.

Visual direction: symbolic tiles from your sheet, informed by Passonneau & Wurman’s Urban Atlas. Ecological reference: Louisiana Sea Grant on water hyacinth. Terrain sources: NOAA C-CAP service, 2021 metadata, USACE defenses. Isometric process reference: Isometric NYC. Pixel assets were generated for this prototype and assembled into a reusable atlas. No reference map artwork is copied.
